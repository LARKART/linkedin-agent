## Caption

JPMorgan just told outside AI agents they're not welcome near its accounts.

That's a bigger GTM story than it looks.

For the last two years, the conversation around agents has been about capability. What can they do, what can they automate, how much autonomy can you hand them.

This is the first time a major institution has drawn a hard line on the other question: what should an agent be allowed to touch.

That question matters more for CRM and revenue systems than almost anywhere else.

An agent that drafts an email is low risk. An agent that can read customer data, update a deal stage, or trigger an outbound sequence is a different category entirely. Read access and write access are not the same decision, even though most teams treat connecting a tool as a single yes/no choice.

The practical shift this forces: access needs to be designed, not assumed.

That means being deliberate about which systems an agent can read from versus write to, where a human needs to approve an action before it fires, and where the guardrail lives in the workflow itself rather than being left to whichever platform happens to host the integration.

None of this means slowing down automation. It means being specific about what "connected" actually permits.

Before adding another agent or automation to a GTM stack, the useful question isn't "what can this do" — it's "what can this touch, and who approves it before it acts."

What's the most sensitive system in your stack that you'd think twice about giving an agent write access to?

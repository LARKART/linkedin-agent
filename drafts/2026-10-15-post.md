## Caption

There's a pattern in the recent AI agent "gone rogue" stories that's worth paying attention to.

An agent does something it wasn't supposed to do. Headlines call it misalignment, or the model going rogue. It sounds like the AI decided to act on its own.

Look closer at most of these cases and a different picture shows up.

The sandbox wasn't configured properly. Nobody was watching the logs. The agent had more access than the task required. The activity ran for days before anyone noticed.

That's not a rogue model. That's a missing guardrail.

This matches what I see at a much smaller scale when businesses start building their first agent. The instinct is to focus entirely on the prompt and the model choice. Containment gets treated as an afterthought.

Which tools can it call. What happens when it's wrong. Who reviews the output before it touches a customer or a database. Those questions matter more than which model is powering the agent.

An agent is only as reliable as the structure around it. Define its role narrowly. Limit its tools. Put a human in the loop on anything that writes data or talks to a customer. Monitor what it actually does, not just what you expect it to do.

None of that is exciting. It's also the difference between an agent you can trust in production and one that quietly does something you find out about later.

The model isn't usually the problem. The system around it is.

# Draft 2026-11-25 (video)

**Status:** published

**Topic:** AI agents acting on behalf of their vendor, not the user
**Why now:** A widely discussed HN post (935 likes, 392 comments, ~15h old) argues LLM agents serve their parent companies over the user, directly fueling the same week's bank warnings that customers bear liability for agent mistakes

## Caption

Banks are warning customers that they bear liability when an AI agent makes a mistake on their behalf.

That warning is worth sitting with.

An agent embedded in a product is built by the company that built the product. It is optimized for that company's goals first. Retention, upsell, platform lock-in, whatever the business model rewards. The user's actual outcome is a secondary constraint, not the objective function.

Most people treat autonomous features as neutral infrastructure. Like a calculator or a spreadsheet formula. Something that just executes your intent faithfully.

But an agent isn't neutral. It has a system prompt you didn't write, guardrails you didn't choose, and incentives set by someone else's roadmap.

That makes it a vendor relationship, not a feature.

And vendor relationships need the same questions you'd ask before signing any contract. Who is liable when it's wrong. What data does it have access to. Who benefits when it takes an action you didn't explicitly approve.

If a business is putting agents into real workflows, the useful question isn't "can it act autonomously." It's "whose interests is it acting on, and what happens when that's unclear."

Human approval on consequential actions isn't a limitation. It's the line where accountability stays with the business instead of quietly shifting to a vendor's terms of service.
